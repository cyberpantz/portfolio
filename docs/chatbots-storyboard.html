Where the lines come from

The frames above show pasted messages arriving with no account of where a visitor gets them. This is the missing affordance: a tray attached to the composer, holding two or three things you could say at this point.

It is styled in the user's voice, not the assistant's — accent-tinted, below the composer, visually nothing like the assistant's quick-reply chips. That separation matters: chips are the assistant offering structured answers (Head / Stomach / Everywhere); the tray is the demo handing you your lines. Two affordances because they are two different things, and conflating them would make the assistant look like it's putting words in your mouth.

Tray vs chips — the rule

Assistant side, left-aligned, short structured answers → chip. User side, attached to the composer, sentence-or-longer → tray line. If it would be odd for the assistant to put those words in your mouth, it belongs in the tray.

Branching, not a rail

Every tray offers a terse option and a messy one that go to genuinely different places — terse gets asked more clarifying questions, messy gets the extraction showcase. Replaying and choosing differently has to be worth it or the tray is just a Next button wearing a costume.

“Your line” — the label I'd test

Theatrical, honest about the simulation, explains itself in two words. The risk is that it wears thin over twenty-five beats. Fallbacks: Say…, You could say, or dropping the label and letting the tray speak for itself after the first use.

Two layers, and the bezel is the border

P1–P4 above draw the tray inside the device for legibility, and that's wrong. A real care navigator has no tray. Everything the demo needs in order to be a demo belongs outside the bezel, because the alternative is that every screenshot of this piece has demo furniture in it — a real cost for something going in a portfolio.

So: inside the bezel is the product and never winks. Outside is the apparatus and never pretends. The apparatus gets its own treatment — mono type, neutral paper, no accent — so the two are never mistaken for each other at a glance.

🔒 invariants

The skin switcher

Skin and scenario are two switches, not one. A scenario is script, voice and options; a skin is tokens. Keeping them orthogonal is what lets the care conversation re-skin in place, mid-sentence, without resetting — which is a far better demonstration than three separate demos, because the copy holds still while everything around it moves.

Try it. Tokens snap; the properties that consume them transition over 420ms. Corners round, colour warms, the rhythm opens up, and the pulse slows — the whole surface morphs rather than cutting.

Why custom properties don't transition

An unregistered custom property is a string to the animation engine, so --s-accent snaps. The fix used here is to let tokens snap and transition the consumers — background-color, border-radius, gap. @property with a <color> syntax would also work; this way is cheaper and degrades better.

What must not morph

Safety states, scroll position, and conversation history. Under prefers-reduced-motion the morph is suppressed entirely — the skin still changes, it just arrives rather than travels.

A switch I'd leave out

Exposing the voice parameter separately — sliding humour 0→1 on the care script — is fascinating for about ten seconds and then reads as a toy. The skin switch already makes the point.

Re-skinning — and the half that refuses to be re-skinned

Same markup, same components, same state machine. A scenario supplies a token block and the whole surface changes — palette, type, radius, density, and motion tempo as a single multiplier, so family breathes 35% slower and feline 20% faster without re-timing a single animation by hand.

But a theming system that can restyle the emergency screen is a bug. Safety states take no custom properties at all — hard-coded values, deliberately un-overridable, so a playful scenario cannot end up with a charming 911 screen. Same for contrast floors, target sizes, reduced-motion, and the button-language rules. The skin owns how it feels; it does not own whether it's safe.

Cross-cutting rules, not per-component

Three things every component obeys, so they never have to be re-litigated: humour lives in prose, never in a control; uncertainty is always an offered answer and routes to the safer branch; every terminal state has a door out of the product.

The safety override

Tempo 1 is the only global state in the system: it withdraws the strip, halts motion, forces humour to 0, and disables the composer. Built once, triggered by scenario data. Everything else is local.

What I'd build first

Thinking, option strip, recovery, safety. They're the four with the most states, the most cross-cutting rules, and the least chance of being right on the second attempt — and three of the four are what the piece is actually about.

What the second and third scenarios broke

1 · “You” was hard-coded

Every string assumed the asker is the subject. A parent gets “your daughter,” a cat owner gets “Mochi.” Pronouns and the subject's name become scenario data, and the engine needs a name-capture turn.

2 · Sensation questions don't generalise

A severity slider needs someone who can feel and report. A cat can do neither. The whole interview has to move to observation and environment — when, where, what changed — which turns out to be a better question set for the other two as well.

3 · Narrowing isn't one thing

Care narrows by urgency, family by fit, feline by cause. The strip survives, but its verb changes — “ruled out” becomes “less likely,” and in the family scenario nothing is eliminated at all, it's ranked. Elimination was an assumption, not a mechanic.

4 · The safety rule isn't always an alarm

911 in seconds; a crisis line today; a vet this week. Same slot, three tempos. The component needs an urgency level, and only the top one gets to stop the interface.

5 · Acknowledgment is mandatory in exactly one

A turn that only says that sounds like a hard few months before proceeding. Saccharine in care, unbearable in feline, non-negotiable in family.

6 · Destruction can be a symptom

Shredding and going outside the box often mean pain, not spite — the same shape as D6, where garbled typing might be a stroke. The “this may not be what you think it is” turn is universal, and it belongs in the engine rather than in any one script.
